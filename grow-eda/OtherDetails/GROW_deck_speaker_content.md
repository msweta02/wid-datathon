# GROW Deck — What Each Slide Means & What to Say

24 slides. For each: the one-line meaning, then a short spoken script (say it in your
own words — this is the gist, not a script to read verbatim). Timing target: ~10–12
minutes for the full deck, so ~30 seconds per content slide, less on dividers.

---

## 1 — Title: "The Production Side of Food Security"
**Meaning:** Sets the frame — GROW viewed as supply-chain risk, production data only.
**Say:** "We took the GROW track — crop production — and pointed it at food-supply risk.
Everything here comes from FAOSTAT production data alone. Think of it as the supply
side of the food-security story: who grows the world's staples, how fragile that is,
and who's falling behind."

## 2 — Headline: "A few crops are one bad harvest from a global shortfall"
**Meaning:** The punchline up front — three striking concentration facts.
**Say:** "Here's our headline finding. India alone is 69% of the world's buffalo milk.
Lose Indonesia's oil palm harvest and 58% of world supply is gone. A third of the
world's soya is one country — Brazil. A handful of crops are dangerously concentrated,
and the rest of this deck shows how we found that and what it means."

---

## 3 — Part 1 divider: "What the world grows, and where"
**Say:** "First, the broad scan — what's grown, where, and how it's changing."

## 4 — "A handful of crops feed the world" (top foods bar)
**Meaning:** Global production by commodity — sugar cane, maize, rice, wheat lead.
**Say:** "A small set of crops dominates global tonnage — sugar cane, maize, rice,
wheat. But tonnage isn't the same as importance: sugar cane and milk are heavy and
wet. So we don't stop at volume — we layer on concentration and value next."

## 5 — "Five countries carry global output" (top countries bar)
**Meaning:** Production is concentrated in a few producer nations.
**Say:** "By weight, five countries — China, India, Brazil, the US, Indonesia — grow
the bulk of the world's food. That concentration of producers is the backdrop to every
supply-risk question that follows."

## 6 — "Value doesn't always track volume" (value line chart)
**Meaning:** Pairing production value with tonnage — the economic lens.
**Say:** "When we bring in economic value, not just tonnage, we see where crops are
shifting toward higher-value output. Most analyses stop at volume; value divergence is
a subtler signal that separates 'grows a lot' from 'grows something worth a lot.'"

## 7 — "Production is shifting between regions" (regional share, aggregates)
**Meaning:** FAOSTAT continent aggregates — each region's share of world output over time.
**Say:** "Using FAOSTAT's continental aggregates, we can watch production shift between
regions over decades. This answers the brief's core question directly: how is food
production moving across the world geographically."

## 8 — "Some crops are one country deep" (concentration / HHI bar)
**Meaning:** HHI concentration index — buffalo milk, oil palm, soya are single-point-of-failure.
**Say:** "This is the concentration measure — the HHI, the same index antitrust
regulators use for market concentration. Above 2,500 is 'highly concentrated.' Buffalo
milk, oil palm and soya are off the charts: their production sits in one or two
countries. Those are single-point-of-failure crops."

## 9 — "Lose one producer, lose the supply" (supply-shock bar)
**Meaning:** % of world supply lost if the #1 producer is removed.
**Say:** "We simulated a shock — knock out the top producer. Lose Indonesia's oil palm
and 58% of world supply disappears overnight; lose Brazil's soya and it's a third. If a
crop barely grows anywhere else, that shock is baked into the geography."

## 10 — "Staple concentration over time" (concentration trend)
**Meaning:** Top-3 producer share tracked across years — is fragility rising?
**Say:** "We also asked whether this is getting worse. Tracking the top-3 producer share
over time tells us if global supply is growing more concentrated. A rising line would
mean systemic risk increasing — a natural thing to model further."

## 11 — "Africa's output is standing still" (self-sufficiency)
**Meaning:** Per-capita production by region — Africa flat, others rising.
**Say:** "On per-capita production, every region climbed over the last decade except
Africa, which stayed flat. With Africa's population growing fast, flat per-capita output
signals rising reliance on imports. Note this is relative to each region's own baseline,
so the point is Africa's flatness, not the absolute number."

---

## 12 — Part 2 divider: "The staples up close: wheat, rice, maize"
**Say:** "Now we zoom from the global scan into the three crops that anchor world food
supply — wheat, rice, and maize."

## 13 — "Wheat, rice and maize since 1961" (staple trend)
**Meaning:** Six decades of production — all three grew, maize pulled ahead.
**Say:** "All three staples roughly quadrupled since 1961. But maize broke away after
2000, overtaking both — driven by feed and industrial demand, not just food on plates."

## 14 — "A tenfold wheat yield gap" (yield distribution)
**Meaning:** Yield spread across countries — huge headroom for laggards.
**Say:** "Wheat yields span a factor of ten — top countries get around 10 tonnes per
hectare, the lowest under one. That gap is an opportunity: closing even part of it lifts
world output without clearing a single new hectare. It's the food-security-positive
finding in the deck."

## 15 — "Where wheat is grown, over time" (wheat regional share)
**Meaning:** Wheat's regional share shifts; no single dominant grower.
**Say:** "Wheat is spread out — its top producer is only about 18% of world output, and
the regional mix has shifted over time. Remember that low concentration; it matters in a
minute when we bridge to trade."

## 16 — "Where rice is grown, over time" (rice regional share)
**Meaning:** Rice is overwhelmingly Asian, stable for decades.
**Say:** "Rice is the opposite story — overwhelmingly Asian. The continent has held
around 90% of world production for decades. That structural concentration shapes how
Asian countries trade rice."

## 17 — "Where maize is grown, over time" (maize regional share)
**Meaning:** Americas lead maize; Asia's share climbing with feed demand.
**Say:** "Maize is led by the Americas, but Asia's share has climbed steadily as demand
for animal feed and industrial use grows worldwide."

---

## 18 — Part 3 divider: "Same crops, same countries, two lenses"
**Meaning:** The bridge — production side of the TRADE deck's wheat/rice findings.
**Say:** "This is where GROW meets TRADE. Same crops, same countries, but from the
production side. If a TRADE team looked at who imports and how exposed they are, we can
show why — from who grows what."

## 19 — "Who grows the wheat others import" (top wheat producers)
**Meaning:** Top wheat producers = the supply base behind importers.
**Say:** "These are the top wheat producers — the supply base every importer depends on.
Key nuance: wheat is grown widely, but exported by very few. Most producers grow it to
feed themselves. So importers still face a handful of suppliers even though production
looks spread out."

## 20 — "Australia's wheat: volatile output" (Australia wheat qty/value)
**Meaning:** A key exporter whose output swings with drought.
**Say:** "Australia is one of the few big wheat exporters — and its production swings
hard with drought. That volatility at the export end is exactly the kind of wobble that
propagates downstream to countries depending on it."

## 21 — "Indonesia grows no wheat" (53 Mt rice vs ~0 Mt wheat cards)
**Meaning:** The root cause — Indonesia grows tons of rice, zero wheat.
**Say:** "Here's the cleanest bridge point. Indonesia is the world's fourth-largest rice
producer — 53 million tonnes — but it grows essentially no wheat. That single contrast
is why it's relaxed about rice and dependent on wheat imports. It grows one and not the
other."

## 22 — "Who grows the world's rice" (top rice producers)
**Meaning:** Rice production concentrates in Asia — India + China ≈ half.
**Say:** "On the rice side, production concentrates in Asia — India and China alone are
nearly half of world output. That Asian concentration shapes who can export and who must
import."

## 23 — "Vietnam's rice: steady surplus" (Vietnam rice qty/value)
**Meaning:** A dependable rice exporter with steadily rising output.
**Say:** "Vietnam is a major rice exporter with steady, rising output — the dependable
supply that flexible importers can lean on. Contrast this with Australia's volatile
wheat: a stable exporter versus a swingy one."

## 24 — "China grows its own rice" (China rice qty/value)
**Meaning:** China's huge domestic rice output = it can import by choice, not need.
**Say:** "And China closes the loop. It grows over 200 million tonnes of its own rice —
which is exactly why it can treat rice imports as optional, not critical. Compare that to
Indonesia and wheat: China grows what it needs, so trade is a choice; Indonesia doesn't,
so trade is a necessity. Same two lenses — production explains the trade behavior."

---

## Closing line (spoken, no slide)
"So that's the supply side of food security: where the world's staples come from, which
crops are dangerously concentrated, and why some countries import out of necessity while
others do it by choice. It stands on its own — and it lines up directly against an
import-side trade analysis."

## If asked tough questions

**"Isn't tonnage a weak measure?"** — "Agreed, which is why we layered concentration,
value, and per-capita indices on top. Tonnage is the scan; the risk metrics are the
analysis."

**"How reliable is the data?"** — "We checked FAOSTAT's quality flags — about 81% of
staple values are official figures. Older data is thinner, so we lean on the recent
period for anything quantitative."

**"Why no trade data?"** — "We kept it GROW-only on purpose, so it complements a TRADE
team rather than duplicating them. Production geography already encodes most of the
supply risk."

**"Is the Africa self-sufficiency claim solid?"** — "It's a proxy. The index is relative
to each region's own baseline, so we read direction and flatness, not absolute levels.
Confirming actual import reliance would need trade data, which we're upfront about."

**"Wheat isn't concentrated in your data — doesn't that contradict the trade vulnerability?"**
— "It actually explains it. Wheat is grown widely but exported by few — most producers
feed themselves. So production looks safe while the tradeable surplus is concentrated.
That's why an importer like Indonesia still faces few suppliers."
